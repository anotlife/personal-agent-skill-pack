# Source Quality and Triangulation

## Practical preference order

1. Primary documents, raw datasets, original research, first-party filings.
2. Official documentation and first-party statements.
3. Reputable secondary analysis (established press, review articles, textbooks).
4. Community reports, when lived experience is the evidence you need.
5. Aggregators, SEO content, and AI-generated summaries — only when nothing better exists, and labelled as such.

Prefer the source **closest to the fact**. If a blog describes a paper, cite the paper. If a paper describes a dataset, the dataset is closer.

## Independence test

Ten pages repeating the same announcement are not ten confirmations — they are one confirmation counted ten times. Look for genuinely different underpinnings:

- different underlying data;
- different authorship and institutions;
- different methodology;
- direct measurement versus reporting of someone else's measurement.

A single press release syndicated across twenty outlets is one source. Track the origin.

## Source-function matching

A source can be authoritative for one question and weak for another.

| Source | Strong for | Weak for |
|---|---|---|
| Official docs | Intended API behaviour, syntax | Real-world reliability, edge cases in practice |
| Vendor benchmark | Their product's design intent | Independent comparison |
| Peer-reviewed study | Effect measured under stated conditions | Generalisation beyond those conditions |
| News article | What was publicly claimed, when | Technical accuracy |
| Community post | A reproducible symptom in the wild | Prevalence, causation |
| Textbook | Established consensus | Anything after its edition date |

Ask "authoritative *for what*" rather than "authoritative".

## Triangulation

Use independent evidence when the claim matters:

- two sources with different origins agreeing;
- a claim plus a mechanism that explains it;
- a measured value plus an order-of-magnitude sanity check;
- a documented behaviour plus an observed run.

Note the asymmetry: agreement across genuinely independent sources strengthens a claim; agreement across sources sharing an origin does not.

## Synthesis when sources disagree

1. Preserve the disagreement. Do not silently pick a side.
2. Identify what each source actually measured or claimed — often they are answering different questions.
3. Compare dates and time windows.
4. Compare definitions and populations.
5. Prefer the source closest to the disputed fact.
6. Report the disagreement and what would resolve it.

Never average incompatible evidence into a fake consensus. "Sources disagree; the primary source says X, a 2024 analysis says Y" is a better answer than a mean of two incomparable numbers.

## Red flags in a source

- No date, no author, no methodology.
- Extraordinary claim with no primary link.
- Numbers with no stated denominator, sample size, or units.
- A source that cites a summary rather than an original.
- Content that exists only to be cited (SEO filler, listicles, AI-written pages).
- A page whose headline states a stronger claim than its body supports.

## Checklist

- [ ] My sources are the closest available to each fact.
- [ ] Agreements I rely on come from independent origins.
- [ ] I matched each source's strength to the question I used it for.
- [ ] I preserved rather than smoothed over disagreements.
- [ ] Nothing I cite is an aggregator of something better.
