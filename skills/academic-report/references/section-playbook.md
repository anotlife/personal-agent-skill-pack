# Section Playbook

## Order of work

Draft in this order, regardless of the order sections appear in the document:

1. **Results** — because everything else must be consistent with what actually happened.
2. **Method** — because the reader must know what produced those results.
3. **Discussion** — the interpretation of those results.
4. **Introduction** — last, because only now do you know what context is actually needed.
5. **Abstract / summary** — last of all.

Drafting the introduction first is how reports end up claiming things the results do not deliver.

## Introduction / objective

State the question, the context needed to understand it, and what will be tested or produced. Cut broad historical filler and textbook recaps of well-known material.

The test: *does every sentence in the introduction help a reader understand why this experiment was done or what its result would mean?* If not, cut it.

## Method

Report what was **actually done**, not an idealized procedure that was intended but not followed. This is where fabrication most often enters academic writing, because the standard protocol is easier to write than the messy actual one.

Cover: materials and quantities, equipment and settings, sample sizes, controls, the sequence of operations, and any deviation from the expected protocol. Deviations are the most valuable content in this section.

## Results

Describe observations and calculated results **before** explaining them. Explanation belongs in the Discussion.

- Present results in a fixed order: raw observations, derived quantities, then statistical summaries.
- Every figure and table is referenced in the text and answers a question.
- State n, the number of repetitions, the controls, and what was excluded and why.
- Report what did not work. A failed run is a result.

## Discussion

Use this sequence for each result:

**result → evidence → plausible explanation → alternative explanation → limitation → next check**

Do not restate the Results section in decorative prose. If a sentence repeats a number from Results without adding interpretation, delete it.

When a result is surprising, preserve the surprise. Explain why it is surprising, offer the candidate explanations, and say what measurement would discriminate between them.

## Conclusion

Answer the stated question at the level the evidence justifies, and nothing more. Do not introduce new evidence, new data, or new sources here.

| Do not write | Write |
|---|---|
| "This proves that X causes Y" | "Within this design, X was associated with Y; isolating a causal effect would require Z" |
| "The experiment was successful" | "The measured value differed from the expected value by 4%; the likely sources are A and B" |
| "Future work could explore many directions" | "Repeating at a third temperature would distinguish the A and B explanations" |

## Figures, tables, and captions

- A caption must be readable without the body text: what is shown, units, n, and what the reader should notice.
- Axes have labels and units. A plot without units is not a result.
- Do not use a table for two values, or a figure where a sentence suffices.
- Do not describe a figure's shape in prose when the figure is right there; describe what it *means*.

## Assignment adaptation

If the instructor provides headings, grading criteria, word limits, a rubric, or a required format, those outrank this playbook in every case. Read the assignment before writing a word.

When there is a word limit, cut in this order: historical background, restated definitions of well-known terms, repeated results, and hedged speculation — never the limitations.

## Checklist

- [ ] I drafted in Results-first order.
- [ ] The Method describes what was done, including deviations.
- [ ] Results contain no interpretation; Discussion contains no new results.
- [ ] The Conclusion adds no new evidence.
- [ ] Every figure and table is referenced and self-explanatory.
- [ ] I followed the assignment's structure rather than a default template.
